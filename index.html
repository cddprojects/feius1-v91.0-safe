The checklist

Read the posting with these four questions

Keep the ad open beside you. When a detail is missing, the line under each step is what to ask.

01 The posting

Is the posting genuine?

Open the employer’s own careers page and look for the same title, the same location, and the job ID when the ad includes one. If the opening is not there, reach the company through the phone number or email published on its website.

Leave if anyone asks you to pay in order to be hired, or sends a check and tells you to return part of the money.

What to ask

“Is this job still open? Where should I apply?”

  • How to spot a job scam (FTC) (opens in a new tab)
  • Where to look for jobs (USAGov) (opens in a new tab)

02 The pay

What would this job pay?

Find an hourly rate, a salary range, or a flat fee if the work is a project. When an ad says “up to $25 an hour,” ask which part is base pay and which part depends on tips, commissions, or bonuses.

When the ad lists no range, your state labor office can tell you which employers are required to show one.

What to ask

“What’s the starting pay for this job at this location?”

  • Find your state labor office (opens in a new tab)
  • New York’s pay disclosure rules (opens in a new tab)

03 The hours

Which hours would you work?

Ask for the days, the shift times, and the usual hours in a week. The words “flexible schedule” can mean you choose when to pick up work, or that a manager needs you on different shifts from week to week. Find out when training happens, and confirm the time zone if the job is remote.

What to ask

“What would a typical week look like? When would I need to be available for training?”

04 The location

Where would the work happen?

For on-site and hybrid roles, get the street address and how often you would need to be there. For remote and online roles, confirm the company hires people in your state, and whether training or meetings require you to appear in person.

What to ask

“Can I do this job from [your state]? Is there anything I’d need to come in for?”

U.S. job market · November 2026

October brought a larger gain in jobs

In October, U.S. employers added 165,000 jobs to payrolls. That is well above the average monthly gain of 48,000 over the prior year. Unemployment was little changed, at 4.1%.

October jobs report from the Bureau of Labor Statistics (opens in a new tab)

Industries that added jobs

Jobs added minus jobs lost in October 2026

+70,000

Professional and business services

Both technical services and administrative support gained jobs. For office roles, search titles such as administrative assistant and operations assistant.

+42,000

Health care

Hospitals, home health agencies, and outpatient centers added jobs. On their careers pages, look for patient scheduler, front desk, and home health aide roles that match what you are qualified to do.

+25,000

Transportation and warehousing

Most of that gain came from courier services. If you want shift work, search “courier” or “package handler” near you, then check the hours.

How to use these numbers

  • Hotels and restaurants are still worth checking. Leisure and hospitality employment barely changed in October. In September, hotels and food-service businesses both hired more people and had more people leave. Look at local employers even when the industry’s overall job count is flat.
  • Read openings and hires side by side. At the end of September, employers had 7.5 million jobs open and hired 5.4 million people during that month. Both counts changed little from August. To see roles you can apply for now, use company careers pages in your area.
  • Ask which hours are dependable. In October, 4.8 million people were working part time because their hours were reduced or they could not find full-time work. That figure rose by 339,000 from September. If you need steady pay, ask whether the employer guarantees a minimum number of hours.

U.S. job search updates

Rules and scam notes to check

Read the date and the place on each note before you compare a posting or an offer.

California and Washington

Starts January 1, 2027

Minimum wage rises in January

Starting January 1, the statewide minimum wage is set to rise to $17.40 an hour in California and $17.73 an hour in Washington. If you are comparing offers for 2027, check the rate where you would work. Some cities and counties set a higher minimum.

  • California labor department announcement (opens in a new tab)
  • Washington labor department announcement (opens in a new tab)

Maine and Virginia

In effect since July 2026

More job ads have to show the pay

Since July 29, 2026, Maine employers with 10 or more employees have had to list the expected pay range in covered job ads, or say when a job pays commission only. Since July 1, 2026, Virginia employers have had to list the wage, salary, or pay range in covered job ads. They also cannot ask applicants about past pay.

If the ad is vague, ask what pay range the company has in mind.

  • Maine Department of Labor update (opens in a new tab)
  • Virginia pay disclosure law (opens in a new tab)

Chicago, Illinois

In effect since July 1, 2026

More Chicago workers may qualify for advance notice of shifts

Chicago’s Fair Workweek rules can apply to workers making up to $33.85 an hour or $64,945.55 a year. Coverage also depends on the industry and the employer. If you are applying for shift work in Chicago, ask whether the job is covered and when you will get your schedule.

  • Chicago Fair Workweek coverage (opens in a new tab)
  • Chicago’s July 2026 labor updates (opens in a new tab)

Nationwide

FTC alert · April 2026

Watch for texts about remote jobs you never applied for

The FTC flagged messages that pitch a vague remote job and ask you to text back “YES” or “INTERESTED.” Do not reply. If you want to see whether the opening is real, go straight to the company’s careers page or call the number on its website.
